1. The Burden and Conflict of "Doing the Right Thing"
The Struggle of Virtue: While the moral path should theoretically be straightforward, Victor Hugo shows that doing the right thing is agonizingly difficult. Valjean faces constant internal resistance when forced to choose between self-preservation and moral duty.

Christian-Rooted Redemption: Redemption in Les Misérables stems from Christian frameworks where sin is defined by self-absorption. Consequently, true virtue requires actively embodying love and compassion—motivating Valjean to commit radical acts like urging Cosette to forgive the Thénardiers, breaking laws to aid others, and agonizing over whether to turn himself in to save Champmathieu.

2. The Practical Paradox of Conscience vs. Self-Preservation
The Allure of Ignorance: You raise a compelling philosophical question: wouldn't life be easier without a conscience? If Valjean had stripped away his guilt, he could have comfortably remained a wealthy, respected mayor without turning himself in, kept Cossette safely isolated with him in the convent, or simply let Marius die.

The Cost of Living a Lie: While a purely self-preserving life is possible, your analysis notes that Valjean would have suffered a slow psychological death. Avoiding his conscience would mean living a lie, whereas leaning into virtue gives him peace of mind and profound existential meaning through his capacity to love others (the poor, Cossette, and eventually Marius).

3. The Shadow Side: Trauma, Guilt, and Self-Loathing
The Corruption of Conscience: Here, you offer a sharp, original critique of Valjean's character. His extreme self-sacrifice isn't only pure virtue; it is deeply poisoned by his 20 years of brutal imprisonment and societal shunning.

Failing to Love Oneself: Because he internalized the belief that he is an unlovable, worthless convict, his conscience demands harsh self-punishment. Even when he finally earns happiness, his self-loathing convinces him that he doesn't deserve to be around Cossette and Marius, leading him to unnecessarily cut himself off from them.

4. Final Philosophical Synthesis
The Dual Nature of Conscience: Your essay concludes with a nuanced takeaway on human morality. Living without a conscience creates an empty existence devoid of love, making virtue meaningful when defined as loving others.

The Ultimate Caveat: However, we must ruthlessly examine our own inner motivations. If our conscience is secretly fueled by unhealed trauma, overwhelming guilt, or self-hatred, we risk becoming enslaved to our own self-punishment—unjustly depriving ourselves of the happiness and love we actually deserve.
